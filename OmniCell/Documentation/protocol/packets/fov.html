Native client proof: MessageProtocol.dll Message_t::CreateDataBlock creates bytes 2–15; Connection.dll Connection_t::Send at RVA 0x16F3 writes bytes 0–1, 4–7; N3.dll n3InfoItemRemote_t::Write at RVA 0x98F4 writes opcode, affected identity, and ToBePassedOn at bytes 16–28. Retail captures independently match those writes. Packet body: Seven of the nine fields are the client's own, and the two that are not are known to be unused rather than merely unobserved. Extracted-client FovIIR_c - note the IIR_c rather than IIR_t, which is why the class name scan never found it - has vtable 0x10161F80 and reader 0x10073BA2, and a bare return for a writer, so the client never sends this. The reader allocates thirty six bytes and fills them with two floats, one byte, five more floats and an int32; thirty six in memory rather than the thirty three on the wire because of the padding after the byte. Nothing in it is conditional. The dispatcher at 0x10073C32 resolves the identity to a dynel, hands the block to the object at dynel + 0x1D8, and sets stat 533, npcfovstatus, to 1. Two functions touch that block and they are what name it. The receiver at 0x100524F4 tidies it: it forces the two turn speeds positive, defaults the current speed to the second of them when it is zero, clamps the byte to 0-2, and - when the centre angle is above zero - throws the sent centre away and works it out from the direction the NPC is already facing, copying the result into the current angle. The sweep at 0x10052332 then runs every frame: it advances the current angle by the current speed times the elapsed time, and when the angle leaves the arc formed by the centre plus and minus the half-angle it takes one of three arms off the byte - pin and reverse, jump to the far end, or stop. Finally it builds a rotation about the up axis by the current angle and hands it to the dynel as its heading. So this message is an NPC turning its head back and forth, and the fields are the arc, the two speeds, and where in the arc it is. The captures agree exactly. Both NPCs carry 0 in the byte, which is the pin-and-reverse arm, and their signed speed is only ever plus or minus their own turn speed, split almost evenly between the signs across 391 copies - which is what that arm does and what neither of the others would. The angles land on whole degrees: 65 and 22.5 for the two arcs, 25 and 20 for the speeds, 234 and 270.5 for the two centres. Only the current angle differs per packet, and it is distinct in all 391. What keeps this off green is the first float and the trailing int32. Between them the two functions above read block offsets 4, 8, 0xC, 0x10, 0x14, 0x18 and 0x1C; offset 0 and offset 0x20 are written by the reader and never read. This entry used to add that the block is not handed to anything else, and that was the wrong reason for a right conclusion - checked again on 2026-09-12, 0x100524F4 does not copy the fields out, it frees whatever was at the receiver's + 0x24 and parks the block there, so the receiver keeps it and any of its methods could reach the two. None does: a sweep of the receiver's whole code region for a load of + 0x24 followed by a read at the block's + 0 or + 0x20 finds nothing. The first is 15 degrees on one NPC and 22 on the other and the int32 is 13 and 20, which is suggestive of a vision cone and a range and is not evidence of one. Naming them on two NPCs would be a guess.
